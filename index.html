Neural thickets survive without weight noise, reshaping model specialization

Rethinking Neural Thickets Without Weight Perturbations

Nicolas Dufour, Elouan Gardès, Amil Dravid · cs.LG · 8/10 signal

Task‑specific competence can be extracted without traversing weight thickets, so pruning and adaptation can focus on activation pathways.

The authors challenge the Neural Thickets hypothesis, which asserts that pretrained language models contain dense clusters of task‑specific experts reachable via small weight perturbations. By systematically removing weight perturbations and probing with linear probes and fine‑tuning, they demonstrate that task performance does not hinge on navigating a thicket of local minima. Their experiments span GPT‑2, LLaMA, and T5 across ten downstream benchmarks, revealing that performance gradients are smoother than previously claimed.

Crucially, they introduce a diagnostic suite—weight‑space curvature analysis, activation‑space clustering, and gradient‑flow tracing—to map where task‑relevant information resides. The results suggest that task specialization is encoded more in activation patterns than in isolated weight regions, prompting a rethink of model interpretability and pruning strategies.

The whole edition, one paper

The problem
The Neural Thickets hypothesis posits that pretrained LMs hide dense neighborhoods of task‑specific experts in weight space, implying that small weight perturbations can uncover specialized subnetworks. This view underpins many pruning, fine‑tuning, and interpretability methods, yet it lacks rigorous empirical validation across model families and tasks.
How it works
The authors replace stochastic weight perturbations with deterministic weight freezes and evaluate linear probing, low‑rank adaptation (LoRA), and full fine‑tuning. They compute Hessian spectra to quantify curvature, perform activation‑space clustering via k‑means on hidden states, and trace gradient flow during adaptation to see if performance gains align with weight movement or activation reshaping. A control suite injects synthetic noise to compare against the no‑noise baseline.
What they measured
Across GPT‑2 (124M–1.5B), LLaMA (7B), and T5 (3B), removing weight perturbations reduced the average downstream accuracy drop from 3.2% (with noise) to 0.4%, while linear probe scores remained within 1% of the noisy baseline. Hessian spectra showed heavy‑tailed distributions but no distinct low‑curvature basins correlating with task performance. Activation clustering revealed task‑specific clusters with silhouette scores of 0.68 versus 0.32 for weight‑only analyses.
Where it breaks
The study focuses on decoder‑only and encoder‑decoder architectures up to 7B parameters; scaling to >30B models may surface different dynamics. Moreover, the diagnostic tools rely on batch‑level statistics, which could miss rare but critical weight configurations. The paper does not address continual learning scenarios where weight drift accumulates over many tasks.
What this unlocks
Practitioners can now prioritize activation‑centric adaptation—e.g., inserting lightweight adapters or prompt tuning—without fearing that hidden weight thickets must be navigated. This simplifies pruning pipelines, reduces the need for expensive weight‑noise searches, and opens a path to more stable, interpretable fine‑tuning across diverse downstream tasks.
