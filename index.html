One policy, million robots: universal control breakthrough

OmniAct: One Policy, One Million Embodiments

Xiang Fan, Yuheng Wang, Jiayuan Yan · cs.RO · 9/10 signal

Embed robot dynamics into a shared latent space to reuse a single policy across heterogeneous platforms.

OmniAct tackles the long‑standing challenge of scaling a single neural policy across a vast diversity of robotic embodiments. By embedding robot-specific kinematic and dynamic descriptors into a shared latent space, the method learns to modulate behavior without retraining per platform. The authors demonstrate zero‑shot transfer from a handful of training robots to thousands of unseen morphologies, preserving task success rates.

The work is built on a hierarchical policy: a high‑level task planner outputs abstract commands, while a low‑level adaptor conditions on a learned embodiment embedding. Training leverages massive multi‑embodiment simulation batches and a contrastive loss that aligns similar dynamics. Results on manipulation and locomotion benchmarks show less than 5% performance drop compared to per‑robot fine‑tuning, a striking scalability claim.

The whole edition, one paper

The problem
Current robot learning pipelines require training a separate policy for each morphology, which is infeasible as the number of commercial and research platforms explodes. Practitioners need a way to deploy a single controller that can adapt to differing joint limits, actuation strengths, and sensor suites without costly retraining.
How it works
OmniAct constructs an embodiment encoder that ingests a robot’s kinematic tree, joint ranges, and inertial parameters, producing a fixed‑dimensional embedding. This embedding conditions both the observation encoder and the policy network via FiLM-like modulation layers. During training, a large pool of simulated robots is sampled each iteration, and a contrastive dynamics loss forces embeddings of robots with similar dynamics to cluster, improving generalisation. The policy itself is a recurrent transformer that outputs torque commands given the modulated observation stream.
What they measured
On a suite of 12 tasks (pick‑place, door opening, quadruped locomotion), OmniAct trained on 50 source robots achieved 92% of the success rate of individually fine‑tuned baselines on 1,000 held‑out robots. The zero‑shot gap was 3.8% on average, and fine‑tuning the same policy on a new robot required only 15 minutes of additional data to close the gap. Ablations showed that removing the contrastive loss increased the gap to 12%.
Where it breaks
The approach relies heavily on high‑fidelity simulation; real‑world transfer suffers from unmodelled compliance and sensor noise, requiring domain randomisation to bridge the gap. Embedding quality degrades for robots with exotic actuation (e.g., soft or continuum) not represented in the training pool. The method also assumes access to full kinematic specifications, which may be unavailable for proprietary platforms.
What this unlocks
Practitioners can now prototype a new robot by merely supplying its CAD‑derived kinematic description and deploy the pre‑trained OmniAct policy for standard manipulation or locomotion tasks. This reduces integration time from weeks to hours and enables rapid fleet‑wide updates where a single policy rollout benefits all hardware variants.
