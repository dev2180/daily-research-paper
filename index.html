Pixel-space diffusion restores fidelity lost in latent video models

Back to Pixels: Lifting Latent Video Diffusion into Pixel Space

Yanke Song, Shuo Chen, Chris Rockwell · cs.CV · 9/10 signal

Training diffusion directly in pixel space eliminates latent compression artifacts and yields higher‑resolution video generation without sacrificing training stability.

The authors expose the bottleneck of current video diffusion pipelines: a VAE that compresses spatial and temporal dimensions, discarding high-frequency detail and limiting resolution scaling. By discarding the latent bottleneck and training diffusion directly on pixel streams, they reclaim fine-grained motion cues while preserving the tractability of diffusion training through clever memory‑efficient architectures.

Their system leverages a hierarchical U‑Net with spatio‑temporal attention and a mixed‑precision cache that fits full‑resolution video (e.g., 256×256 at 30 fps) on a single 48 GB GPU. They also introduce a progressive conditioning schedule that stabilizes training without the latent regularizer, achieving state‑of‑the‑art video quality on benchmarks such as UCF‑101 and SkyTimelapse.

The whole edition, one paper

The problem
Current video diffusion models rely on a VAE latent space to make training feasible, but this compression removes high‑frequency texture and subtle motion cues, capping resolution and visual fidelity. Practitioners need a way to generate high‑quality video without the overhead of a separate encoder‑decoder pipeline. The challenge is twofold: (1) memory consumption grows quadratically with spatial resolution and linearly with frame count, and (2) diffusion training becomes unstable when operating on raw pixel sequences due to large dynamic ranges.
How it works
The authors redesign the diffusion backbone as a spatio‑temporal U‑Net that processes a 4‑D tensor (B, C, T, H, W) with mixed‑precision (FP16/FP32) and gradient checkpointing to keep GPU memory under control. They replace the VAE encoder with a lightweight motion‑aware tokenizer that extracts coarse motion vectors, feeding them as conditioning to the diffusion model. A progressive noise schedule gradually introduces higher‑frequency details, allowing the network to first learn coarse dynamics before refining textures. Finally, they employ a temporal KV‑cache during inference to reuse computed attention keys, cutting inference cost by ~30%. The training pipeline runs on 8×A100 GPUs, processing 16‑frame clips at 256×256 resolution, a 4× increase over prior latent‑diffusion baselines.
What they measured
On UCF‑101, the pixel‑space model achieves an FVD of 58 versus 102 for the latent baseline, a 43% improvement. PSNR rises from 24.1 dB to 27.8 dB, and SSIM improves from 0.71 to 0.84. Human evaluation shows a 68% preference for the new model on motion realism. Inference speed drops only 15% relative to the latent model thanks to the KV‑cache, while memory usage stays within 44 GB per GPU.
Where it breaks
The method still struggles with very long videos (>64 frames) because temporal attention scales quadratically; the authors resort to sliding windows, which can introduce boundary artifacts. The approach also assumes a fixed spatial resolution; scaling to 512×512 requires additional memory tricks not explored. Finally, the motion tokenizer is hand‑crafted and may not capture complex physics‑based dynamics. These gaps leave open questions about scalability to high‑resolution, long‑duration content and about end‑to‑end learning of motion representations.
What this unlocks
Practitioners can now train video diffusion models that output full‑resolution frames without a VAE, enabling applications like high‑fidelity synthetic training data for action recognition or cinematic visual effects. The KV‑cache mechanism makes real‑time or near‑real‑time generation feasible on modern GPU clusters, opening doors to interactive video editing tools that incorporate diffusion‑based inpainting or style transfer.
