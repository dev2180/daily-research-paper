Meta‑learned parameters expose additive compositional analogies

Linking Rapid Learning to Compositional Structure in Neural Networks: Evidence from Additive Analogies in Meta-Learned Parameter Spaces

Abigail L. Tenenbaum, R. Thomas McCoy · cs.LG · 9/10 signal

Inspecting and manipulating meta‑learner weights reveals a reusable algebraic substrate for rapid task composition.

The authors probe why meta‑learning yields rapid adaptation, hypothesizing that the learned parameter space encodes compositional structure. By training a meta‑learner on few‑shot tasks and then analyzing its weight updates, they discover linear relationships that correspond to semantic analogies across tasks. This bridges the gap between fast learning and the compositionality observed in human cognition.

Through a series of controlled experiments on synthetic and vision‑based few‑shot benchmarks, they demonstrate that additive vector operations in the meta‑parameter space predict performance on novel tasks, effectively allowing the model to “compose” previously learned solutions. The work reframes meta‑learning as a form of latent algebraic reasoning rather than mere gradient descent acceleration.

The whole edition, one paper

The problem
Standard deep networks require massive data and training time to acquire new skills, whereas humans can learn from a handful of examples by leveraging compositional knowledge. Existing meta‑learning methods achieve few‑shot performance but offer little insight into *how* they generalize so quickly, limiting their practical deployment in rapidly changing domains.
How it works
The authors train a Model‑Agnostic Meta‑Learning (MAML) framework on a distribution of tasks, then freeze the meta‑parameters and treat each task’s fine‑tuned weights as points in a high‑dimensional space. By performing linear regressions and vector arithmetic on these points, they uncover consistent additive directions that correspond to specific task attributes (e.g., adding a rotation or a color shift). They formalize this as a compositional basis and show that new tasks can be approximated by linear combinations of known task vectors, bypassing gradient steps altogether.
What they measured
On the Omniglot few‑shot classification benchmark, the compositional reconstruction predicts test‑task performance within 2% of the fully fine‑tuned model using only a single linear combination. On Mini‑ImageNet, the method achieves 62.3% accuracy on 5‑way 1‑shot tasks compared to 64.1% for full MAML, a 2.8% gap while cutting adaptation time by 85%. Ablation studies confirm that the additive structure persists across architectures and optimizer settings.
Where it breaks
The discovered linear relationships rely on a relatively homogeneous task distribution; performance degrades when tasks are highly dissimilar or when the meta‑training set lacks sufficient coverage of the target attribute space. Moreover, the method assumes access to the full fine‑tuned weight vectors, which may be impractical for very large models without additional compression tricks.
What this unlocks
Practitioners can now pre‑compute a library of task vectors and compose them on‑the‑fly to adapt models to new specifications without costly gradient updates. This enables near‑real‑time personalization of vision or language systems in edge settings, where compute budgets are tight but a repertoire of known task transformations exists.
