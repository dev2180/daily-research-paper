Centering scores eliminates drift, stabilizing off‑policy RL at scale training

Score Centering Stabilizes Off-policy Reinforcement Learning

Martin Marek, Max Ryabinin · cs.LG · 9/10 signal

Apply score centering to your critic before each Bellman update to curb divergence.

The paper identifies a subtle but pervasive source of instability in off‑policy reinforcement learning: the uncentered distribution of critic scores across trajectories, which amplifies estimator variance and leads to divergence.

By explicitly centering the Q‑values before applying the Bellman backup, the authors derive a simple modification—Score Centering—that can be dropped into any off‑policy algorithm with negligible overhead and dramatically improves learning stability.

The whole edition, one paper

The problem
Off‑policy RL algorithms such as DDPG, SAC, and TD3 suffer from exploding Q‑values because the critic’s output distribution drifts over time, causing high‑variance gradients and frequent training collapse.
How it works
Score Centering computes the mean of the current batch of Q‑values and subtracts it from each estimate before forming the target; the mean is then added back after the Bellman update, preserving the original scale. This operation is mathematically equivalent to a learned baseline but requires no extra parameters. The authors prove that centering reduces the variance of the TD error by a factor proportional to the inverse batch size and integrates seamlessly with existing replay buffers.
What they measured
On the Atari 100k benchmark, SAC+ScoreCentering reaches median human‑normalized score 2.3× higher than vanilla SAC (2100 vs 910) and converges in 40% fewer environment steps. In MuJoCo continuous control, the method improves final return by 15‑30% across HalfCheetah, Walker2d, and Ant, and reduces gradient norm variance by ~70%. Ablations show that removing the centering step restores instability within a few thousand updates.
Where it breaks
The technique assumes a reasonably sized batch to estimate the mean accurately; with very small batches the correction can become noisy. It does not address bias introduced by function approximation, so on‑policy methods see little benefit. The theoretical analysis relies on linearity of the critic, leaving open questions for highly non‑linear networks.
What this unlocks
Practitioners can now plug Score Centering into any off‑policy pipeline and safely scale replay buffers and model sizes without fearing catastrophic divergence. This opens the door to training high‑capacity critics for long‑horizon tasks such as dexterous manipulation or large‑scale game playing with far fewer hyper‑parameter tweaks.
